Why one job
all semester?

A department walkthrough of the Otter Bend simulation: what it changes, what it preserves, and how it turns AI fluency into engineering judgment.

SEIS 201 is not a tour of AI tools. It is practice making engineering decisions when a fast, fluent system can be useful, wrong, or both. A continuous job gives each choice a later consequence—and gives students repeated chances to answer the question that matters: How do you know that is right?

The commitment

The syllabus stays in charge.

The simulation keeps the course’s AI foundations, applied statistics, AI-assisted coding, ethics, exams, project, reflection, and grading weights. Otter Bend is the connective tissue, not a replacement curriculum.

Honest build status

Pilot, not finished product.

Unit 1 meetings M01–M06 and Unit 2 meetings M07–M12 are fully built with Cloud-approved story/SAY. M08 includes its regression/confound evidence and HW4; M09 includes false precision and sampling; M10 adds the confidence-interval / signal-versus-noise hunt; M11 adds a deterministic five-tile dashboard, review form, and Diane’s SKEPTIC / Cloud-PASS SAY; M12 adds a gated chart critique, HW6 redraws, and separate spoiler-safe Exam 1 review. Each built meeting includes decks, handouts, an answer key, and a run-of-day page. M13–M14 remain detailed plans. M12’s passed blocks total 80 minutes against a 75-minute slot, so its run-of-day flags an unresolved timing accommodation. Units 3–5 remain mapped at outline level. Technical values, scenarios, and assessments still require engineering/statistics review before use.

Why teach it this way

Tools change.
Judgment transfers.

A flat tools survey can age as soon as interfaces change. The course instead rehearses durable moves: inspect the source, characterize uncertainty, test against a known answer, document assumptions, and decide what still needs checking.

Consequences create memory.

One Otter Bend project file follows students across the term. Work accepted in February can return in April. That delayed callback makes traceability, units, and honest decision logs matter in a way an isolated worksheet cannot.

One asset holds CE and ME.

The fictional lift bridge supplies civil questions—deck, girders, load rating, traffic, concrete—and mechanical questions—motors, gearbox, bearings, ropes, fatigue, vibration, and thermal movement. Department review can rebalance examples without changing the learning sequence.

The machine has a visible role.

FOREMAN is fast, fluent, and wrong in designed ways. Its arithmetic may be correct while its source, assumption, sample, unit, or conclusion is not. Orange is reserved for the machine; blue identifies people and engineering judgment.

The story is removable.

No graded answer depends on remembering a character or accepting the fiction. A student who ignores the storyline performs the same calculations, source checks, data analysis, and verification work. Each unit ends with a mask-off debrief.

The job in five moves

Unit 1Hired

AI foundations, measurement, descriptive statistics. The first verification note is one sentence.

Unit 2Doubt

Bias, regression, false precision, intervals, charts, and a same-day permit decision.

Unit 3Build

AI-generated Python, tests that can pass while logic is wrong, hand-calculation checks, debugging.

Unit 4Own

Iterative design, calculation tools, research claims, and a rehabilitate-or-replace recommendation.

Unit 5Sign

Risk, independent verification, professional responsibility, limits, and a public-facing briefing.

Who benefits—and how

For students

Practice, not just recognition

  • Use statistics and AI concepts on a coherent engineering record.
  • Transfer a method from the in-class case to unfamiliar homework.
  • See earlier assumptions return, then correct them without hiding the record.
  • Build confidence through checks, not through a tool’s tone.
For the department

A visible spine for review

  • CE and ME examples sit in named slots that faculty can inspect or swap.
  • Scripted errors, clean exam files, and common rubrics improve consistency.
  • The simulation mechanics can be removed if the pilot shows they distract.
  • Open technical-review points are explicit rather than buried.
For PE-bound engineers

A record they can defend

  • Separate a tool’s recommendation from the engineer’s decision.
  • Show sources, assumptions, units, uncertainty, independent checks, and limits.
  • Practice refusing unsupported certainty in safety-relevant work.
  • Treat AI assistance as work that remains under human responsibility.

Expected outcomes

These are grounded in the course description, weekly schedule, and design plan. They translate those documents into observable student performance; they are not new accreditation claims.

Explain modern AI at a working level.Distinguish rule-based and learned-pattern systems; explain next-token generation and agentic loops; identify why fluent output can still be wrong.
Reason statistically about engineering data.Describe spread and uncertainty; interpret distributions, correlation, regression, intervals, and significance without false precision.
Audit AI claims against evidence.Check summaries against source text, question training coverage, detect misleading charts, and trace errors through a data pipeline.
Build and verify calculation tools.Use AI-assisted Python to generate, test, and debug engineering calculations, then compare results with known answers and hand calculations.
Use AI in open-ended design work.Apply AI to research, alternatives, parameter sweeps, and design tradeoffs while keeping requirements and evidence visible.
Document professional judgment.State what was checked, what was not, what assumptions remain, and why the engineer—not the tool—owns the decision.
Recognize limits, bias, and risk.Identify distribution shift, out-of-domain use, automation risk, and situations where AI should not be used.
Communicate a defensible recommendation.Present a concise technical decision to engineering and non-engineering audiences with uncertainty intact.

The recurring evidence artifact is the decision log. It records the item, action, fictional project hours, what was checked, and what was accepted unverified. The “hours” force prioritization but are never grade points.

The verification note escalates with instruction: one source-check sentence in Unit 1; claim/check/result in Unit 2; an independent check and unverified-items line in Units 3–4; a traceable verification record in Unit 5.

Questions Shannon may get

Is a simulation rigorous, or is this gamification?

The rigor is in the unchanged calculations, statistics, source checks, code tests, and applied-practical assessments. The story creates continuity and consequences. No grade depends on role-play, lore, speed, or pleasing Diane; every unit ends with a direct debrief.

Does the bridge make this too civil-heavy for mechanical students?

That is a real design risk, not a solved claim. A lift bridge was chosen because it has both a structure and machinery. Units 1–2 currently lean civil, while machinery supplies measurement, gears, bearings, fatigue, vibration, and thermal behavior. M04, M08, and M10 have identified mechanical alternatives, and the department should help set the final balance.

Are students replacing hand calculations with AI?

No. Students first encounter traceable rules and physical measurement, then use known answers, units, order-of-magnitude checks, and hand-calculation cards to test generated code. A script running cleanly—or tests passing—is explicitly not sufficient evidence.

How does this address PE ethics without overstating policy?

The course teaches the bounded principle supported by its design documents: using AI does not transfer the engineer’s responsibility. Students document sources, checks, assumptions, tool use, and limits. State-specific definitions, stamp language, and technical claims remain review items; this page makes no accreditation or licensure guarantee.

How can grading be fair if students make different decisions?

Common evidence, clean exam files, and a shared rubric score the reasoning and record. The fictional hour budget is not points. A defensible “yes with conditions” and a defensible “no” can both earn full credit if each follows the evidence and states uncertainty.

How are spoilers controlled?

Student and instructor assets are separated. M02 and M04–M12 use student decks first and gated instructor reveals only after students inspect the evidence and commit a decision. Instructor keys, planted conditions, M06’s traffic-record resolution, M07’s applicability conclusion, M08’s temperature-corrected result, M09’s supported reporting form, M10’s interval sensitivity, M11’s designed dashboard failures, M12’s chart dispositions and model redraws, and later callbacks stay outside student navigation and LMS packages.

Why use a separate Case B for homework?

Repeating Otter Bend can reward memory of the reveal. A second fictional system tests transfer: can students recognize the same measurement and averaging failure in unfamiliar data? The M02 Case B is built; later transfer cases should be added only as their source materials and keys are completed.

Does the simulation replace lecture?

No. The current 75-minute pattern combines an inbox prompt, explicit teaching, guided work on the project file, and a written verification note. The scenario supplies the question; instruction supplies concepts and methods; the debrief names what happened.

What if the storyline distracts students—or the first pilot struggles?

Every mechanic is designed to be removable without changing the underlying lab. Students may ignore the fiction. The plan calls for piloting one rich week before building the full semester, watching especially for bookkeeping burden, story overhead, and difficulty separating content problems from simulation problems.

Is the complete semester ready to teach?

No. Unit 1 M01–M06 and Unit 2 M07–M12 are built with Cloud-PASS SAY, including Diane’s SKEPTIC / Cloud-PASS M11 SAY. M13–M14 remain meeting-level plans, and M12’s 80-minute passed clock still needs an approved accommodation in the 75-minute course slot. Units 3–5 remain outlines. Engineering values, data, exam scenarios, CE/ME balance, prerequisite assumptions, and calendar changes still need review or approval.

What the department can improve now

Highest-value review

Interrogate the engineering, not the premise.

Check whether the structure, working values, simplified calculations, fatigue example, permit scenario, and CE/ME distribution are technically fair for this audience.

Pilot questions

Watch the students’ behavior.

Do they transfer the method? Does the decision log reveal reasoning? Does the story clarify stakes without adding cognitive load? Can a mechanic be removed cleanly when it does not help?
